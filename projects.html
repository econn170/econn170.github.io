reassessing the art reference collection in the digital age

description

for my collection development class, i participated in a semester-long project analyzing the reference collection at the moma library, where i was completing a fellowship at the time.

methods

the project was structured over the semester into several assignments: an exploratory phase to understand the library, its audience, and its materials; an acquisition exercise; and a weeding exercise. for the final project, i chose to continue with the moma reference collection analysis, building upon previous assignments and conducting field research on how other art libraries manage their collections.

for the weeding exercise, i analyzed 100–200 books using crew and mustie criteria, then went on to weed around 100 more books to develop a fuller picture of the reference section. i conducted interviews with reference librarians at the met watson library and the frick, with a frequent auction house researcher at moma, and with a current moma employee and a former fellow who had weeded the section previously. i synthesized all findings — from physical observation to user research — and delivered a final presentation with both high-level and granular recommendations.

role

completed independently.

rationale

this project gave me a comprehensive understanding of collection development practice. watching it build upon itself throughout the semester allowed me to see how weeding, analysis, and acquisition all go hand in hand. the guiding question was: what is the role of the art reference section in the 21st century? the field research component — interviews and observations at moma and comparative libraries — pushed me to understand the needs and motivations of both staff and patrons directly.

the normalization of unease: genai use by ischool students

description

in the spring of 2025, i took professor irene lopatovska's human centered research design course, where we spent the semester planning, designing, executing, and writing a research study on graduate student behaviors and attitudes toward genai tools. our paper was published in the information and learning sciences journal in february 2026, and a co-authored version was accepted to the information seeking in context conference (isic) 2026.

methods

the class was highly collaborative. we brainstormed research topics together and landed on the question of how graduate students currently use and feel about genai platforms. we wrote and distributed a survey to pratt ischool students, then invited participants to a focus group, which i helped facilitate. we asked students to draw a representation of how they think genai works, then discussed their drawings and probed further on usage and attitudes. i contributed to coding the interviews, writing up findings, and presenting at the pratt infoshow.

role

collaborative project; primary contributions in focus group facilitation, infoshow presentation, and paper writing.

rationale

this project gave me end-to-end experience with an academic research study and exposure to a range of methods. it also asked me to hold multiple user perspectives simultaneously — the users of genai tools, and the users of our own research instruments — and to stay alert to the biases shaping our inquiry. the divergence between ixd and lis students was itself instructive: researcher positionality mattered as much as any methodological choice.

cult, camp, & coming of age: a resource blog for teens

description

for my literature/literacy for teens class, i created a resource blog on the site builder cargo that introduces teens to cult movies and camp aesthetics through a curated collection of books, films, and websites.

methods

i began by thinking carefully about what an entry point into cult and camp might look like for someone without prior exposure to either concept. because "teen" encompasses an enormous range of interests, reading levels, and media habits, i structured the blog across multiple formats: books for readers, films for movie-watchers, and websites for the digitally curious, with an orienting "start here" section defining key terms in accessible language.

i wrote annotations in a voice that felt direct and conversational without being condescending — the tone i would have wanted as a teen discovering these things for the first time. i also made intentional aesthetic choices on cargo to reflect the campy, irreverent sensibility of the content itself, so that the design would signal to teen users what kind of space this was before they even started reading.

role

sole creator.

rationale

this project required me to think about teenagers as a specific user community while resisting the temptation to treat that community as monolithic. by offering books, films, and websites as parallel entry points into the same topic, i worked to meet users where they were. introducing teens to cult and camp also required careful ethical curation — these genres carry long histories of transgression, queerness, and outsider identity, and i wanted to honor that legacy without flattening it. i thought carefully about queer kids as a primary audience while keeping the site open and welcoming to everyone.

machine divinity

description

machine divinity is a research-creation project in which i scraped eschatological and spiritually-coded text from four 4chan message boards (/pol/, /x/, /lit/, and /g/), categorized it according to petersen & baun's typology of conflictual, revelatory, and resignationist eschatologies, and used it to fine-tune three separate language models. the resulting site allows users to interact with three "digital oracles," each one embodying a distinct eschatological voice distilled from the corpus.

methods

i collected thread data using the 4chan public api and cleaned and filtered the corpus using a custom lexicon of religious, occult, metaphysical, and psychosis-coded terminology. i sorted posts into three eschatological categories, generated sentence embeddings and umap visualizations to explore how these linguistic worldviews clustered, and used each subset to fine-tune a gpt-neo 125m model via huggingface and trl's sfttrainer. i deployed the models through a fastapi backend and built a github pages interface where users can choose an oracle and generate text.

role

sole creator.

rationale

the central question animating machine divinity is an ethical one: what does it mean to archive, read, and dwell inside language that wants you gone? dominant information science responses to harmful online speech tend toward documentation, flagging, or removal — legitimate professional gestures, but ones that position the information worker as a corrective agent and leave something unexamined. i wanted to propose a different posture: what i call archival metabolization. rather than neutralizing or removing this language, i wanted to transform it — to understand what fine-tuning reveals about the essence of a text.

drawing on donna haraway's concept of affinity politics, i argue that a queer archival ethics might orient itself toward understanding rather than distance, and that staying in the difficulty is a legitimate and necessary information practice. the site includes a dedicated ethics statement and process page, reflecting my belief that research-creation work with harmful content carries an obligation to account for itself openly.

odd collage: a digital-physical art platform for children

description

for my growing up digital course, i designed and prototyped odd collage, a hybrid digital-physical art platform for children ages 6–13 where kids can create, upload, remix, print, and share collages in a playful, community-driven environment. the platform is designed for deployment in children's libraries, after-school programs, or school media centers, and pairs with a companion scanner/printer device that allows children to move fluidly between physical and digital making.

methods

the project began as a quick in-class "bag of stuff" prototype developed collaboratively with two classmates, which i then developed into a fully realized digital prototype independently. i started with informal user research — observing a three-year-old i nanny interact with different digital tools and working with him on canva — which expanded my thinking about the age range the platform could serve.

i used resnick's framework of low floors, wide walls, and high ceilings as a guiding design principle: i wanted the platform to be immediately intuitive, open-ended, and forgiving of mistakes. key decisions included replacing likes and comments with a community tagging system to avoid replicating hierarchical social dynamics, building remix and print affordances that link artworks together in a traceable chain, and making the create page entirely icon-based to serve the full age range without relying on reading ability.

role

final prototype designed independently, building on an initial collaborative ideation activity.

rationale

this project asked me to apply technology design principles to a demanding user community where every choice had to be evaluated against developmental appropriateness, emotional safety, and accessibility across a wide age range. i drew on constructionist learning theory (papert, resnick) and youth-centered digital well-being research (ito et al., boyd) to ground my design decisions in evidence. designing for ages 6–13 also meant designing for a community that cannot always articulate its own needs — a six-year-old and a twelve-year-old have radically different reading levels, motor skills, and social relationships to digital platforms.
